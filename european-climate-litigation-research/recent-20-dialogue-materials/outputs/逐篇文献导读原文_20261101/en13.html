Abstract
Following the International Court of Justice ’s (ICJ) Advisory Opinion on the Obligations of
States in Respect of Climate Change , not only have States ’ obligations and responsibilities
regarding climate change come into greater focus, but so too have those of private corporate
greenhouse gas (GHG) emitters. The ICJ opinion adds to an array of recent legal and
regulatory developments that are shaping the accountability of corporate actors for climate
actions and impacts. This article aims to provide a basis for critical analysis of the role of law
and regulation in contributing to global corporate climate accountability, thus responding to a
significant gap in the literature and practice. Underpinned by greater appreciation of carbon
major companies’ role in driving climate change—and growing scientific evidence attributing
specific impacts to their emissions —the article argues that a legal ‘ecosystem’ for global
corporate climate accountability is emerging and seeks to map its core characteristics and
inflection points. Developments across this ecosystem are taking place at and between the
levels of international, domestic/regional and transnational law, including the ICJ’s advisory
ruling, transnational climate cases, harmonised standard-setting processes and findings in
domestic and regional corporate climate litigation. As interactions between these legal
developments increase, the resulting ecosystem of applicable norms and implementation
mechanisms is becoming denser. This article discusses hypothesised implications of these
interactions across the global corporate climate accountability ecosystem, asking to what
extent the ecosystem ’s growing density may amplify corporate accountability for climate
harms or, conversely, dilute companies ’ accountability for the climate effects of their
activities by fragmenting the legal response across a multitude of forums.

2. Corporate climate accountability: concepts and drivers
While the impacts of climate change manifest at different geographic scales (global,
national, local),21 the climate system operates through regional and global atmospheric
processes,22 meaning the causes of and responsibility for these changes are not similarly
localised. Discussions of accountability for climate impacts have traditionally focused
on States, reflecting their primary role both as the subjects of international climate
obligations, such as those under the Paris Agreement,
23 and the source of laws and
regulatory measures shaping the behaviour of private entities like companies operating
in States ’ territories or under their jurisdiction or control.
24 In international law, for
example, the conventional position has been that ‘international law does not matter for
corporations if not through the State ’.25 However, these assumptions are being
challenged by the increasing understanding of the important place of corporations in
processes of global climate change. 26
The following section explores some of the principal factors driving the turn toward
corporate climate accountability, including a greater focus on climate issues in domestic
and regional corporate legal frameworks, increasing climate litigation against corporate
actors and developments in climate attribution science that could buttress arguments
seeking to link corporate GHG emissions to particular climate harms. Acknowledging
that this is a nascent and fast-evolving area of legal scholarship, the section first briefly
discusses different concepts of ‘the corporation’ and ‘corporate accountability’. These
understandings provide foundations for the notion of climate accountability of
corporations employed in this article and discussion in later sections of relevant legal
and regulatory developments.

3.3. Transnational legal activity node
Corporate climate accountability norms are also developing in a transnational node that
sits between, and across, the international and domestic levels. These transnational
processes occupy the space between top-down international laws and bottom-up
domestic regulation and respond, in part, to the transnational nature of climate
change impacts and their attribution to different actors.
The field of transnational law is now well-established but continuously evolving to
address the limits of the traditionally State-centric focus of international law —limits
which have constrained a global approach to both corporate accountability and climate
accountability.
138 The transnational aspect highlights activities and interactions that
transcend national boundaries, without limiting participants to States, thereby
acknowledging increasingly complex global connections and diverse cross-border
actors. In his early formulation, for instance, Jessup described transnational law as
‘all law which regulates actions or events that transcend national frontiers ’.
139
Furthermore, a transnational framing broadens the scope of inquiry beyond formal
international law, such as that described in Section 3.1 , to include non-legal norms,
institutional initiatives and corporate self-regulation. Methodologically, a transnational
approach allows ‘an analytic means for assessing transnationally induced change in a
globalized world’.140
Understanding of transnational legal and regulatory processes continues to broaden
in response to changing historical, social and political contexts, 141 with corporate
climate accountability extending the field to include other complex legal regimes and
new and emerging multi-scalar actors. Corporate climate accountability norms are
developing in this transnational node in a multitude of ways, including by drawing on
transnational litigation outcomes, soft law, informal law or other forms of international
standard-setting and regulation.

5. Conclusion
This article has described the context for a greater focus on corporate climate
accountability and mapped interacting and intersecting legal and regulatory
developments at multiple levels of governance—from the domestic to the international
—that are helping to generate a global legal and regulatory ecosystem. It is argued that
such developments are accompanied by increasing interactions between and across
international, domestic/regional and transnational ‘nodes’ of activity. These nodal
interactions are multi-directional and include international judicial decisions and soft
law implementation, transnational and domestic or regional litigation, domestic and
transnational coalition building and norm diffusion, the development of domestic and
regional regulatory frameworks, and harmonised standard-setting.
At this early stage of the development of the research field and practice of global
corporate climate accountability, it is unclear whether growing density and interactivity
of the emerging regulatory and legal ecosystem may contribute to systemic properties
that enhance accountability for companies’ climate action or simply provide means for
its ultimate dilution. The working theory proposed by the article—to be tested in further
research (that of the authors and hopefully also other interested scholars and
practitioners)—is that greater legal and regulatory density and interactivity across the
system will generate systemic benefits for cor porate climate accountability. Conversely,
reducing legal and regulatory development or rollback—with concomitant decreases in the
interactivity across system nodes—might be expected to have negative consequences for the
climate accountability of corporate actors.

corporate climate accountability, the role of transnational climate advocacy and litigation
seeking remedies for corporate climate harms in driving greater accountability, and the
implications of international climate rulings on States’ responsibilities for regulating private
conduct for enhancing domestic and regional corporate climate action. There is also scope

different corporate forms such as State-owned enterprises or family firms.
By contributing to the mapping of an emerging, multi-nodal accountability
ecosystem, the intention of this article has been to provide a basis for critical analysis
of global corporate climate accountability and the role of law and regulatory
developments in contributing to that objective. It is possible that already, with the
developments to date, the world is witnessing a gravitational pull of law and regulation
towards enhanced corporate climate accountability, which will strengthen over time.
